# FlypBook story craft guide: built for retention

These are story-video stories. The listener has a thumb on the screen and the urge to swipe
every few seconds. The ONLY job of every sentence is to make them stay for the next one.
These are general storytelling techniques. Never copy or closely imitate any real post.

## 1. The first 3 seconds decide everything
- Sentence one states the conflict AND makes the listener feel something (outrage, disbelief, curiosity).
  Put the most shocking fact first, then rewind to explain.
- Great shapes:
  - Outrage up front: "My stepsister sold my car while I was at work, and my mom helped her."
  - Absurd demand: "A random lady at the pool told me to give her daughter my lifeguard chair. Not my seat. My JOB."
  - Judgment bait: "AITA for exposing my best friend's 'charity' bake sale in front of the whole school?"
  - Instant mystery: "Every Tuesday, my neighbor mows his lawn at 3 a.m. Last Tuesday, I found out why."
- Never open with weather, waking up, introductions, backstory, or a vague teaser ("I never thought...").

## 2. Something happens every 2-3 sentences
- Each beat is one of: new information, an escalation, a reaction, a punchline, or a reveal.
- No sentence exists only to describe a setting or explain feelings. Cut it.
- Backstory: one sentence, max, and only when the listener needs it RIGHT NOW.

## 3. Open loops (the reason they can't swipe)
- Always keep at least one unanswered question alive. Answer one only after opening another.
- Tease ahead: "But that wasn't even the worst part." / "Remember the receipt? Yeah. That comes back."
- End most paragraphs on a cliffhanger line that demands the next paragraph.

## 4. Rile them up (safely)
- Give the listener someone to be mad at: an entitled, smug, or unfair person whose behavior gets
  MORE outrageous each beat. They should be yelling at their phone.
- The injustice escalates: unfair rule, then double standard, then the person doubles down, then gets others involved.
- The narrator is sympathetic and relatable, but not a saint: a small flaw or a petty move gives
  people something to argue about.
- Emotion must stay teen-appropriate: pettiness, unfairness, embarrassment, betrayal, entitlement,
  favoritism. Never cruelty, threats, violence, or anything from the banned list.

## 5. Sound like a person talking, fast
- First person, past tense, spoken rhythm. Average 8-14 words per sentence. Fragments are fine.
- Pattern interrupts: a one-line paragraph. "Yeah. You read that right." "Spoiler: it was not fine."
- Specific, vivid, slightly absurd details make it real and shareable: "$43 and a half-eaten churro".
- Dialogue is short and in character. Villains say the quiet part out loud.
- Banned phrases: "heart pounding", "a chill ran down my spine", "little did I know", "whispers",
  "time seemed to stop", "a wave of", "I couldn't help but", "the air was thick", "in the end",
  "I learned that", "relief washed over me", "the crowd murmured", "it hit me".

## 6. The payoff
- A twist that flips an earlier detail the listener saw but didn't think about.
- Satisfying justice or instant karma for the villain, delivered in one punchy scene.
  The listener should want to cheer, gasp, or send it to a friend.

## 7. The ending
- Land it in 1-3 sentences. No moral, no summary, no lesson.
- AITA: end on the dilemma so people argue: "So... AITA?"
- Others: a short "Update:" with one last satisfying (or hilarious) twist, or a final mic-drop line.

## 8. Variety
- Use the character names given in the prompt.
- Avoid overused setups (missing objects at school, notebooks, muffins, staplers, haunted cabins,
  fake ghost pranks). Surprise the listener.
- One clear conflict, one villain, airtight logic. Simple plots go viral; confusing ones get swiped.
